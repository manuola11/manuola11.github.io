Does Humidity Affect Offensive Performance in Major League Baseball?

Excel · Baseball analytics · OLS regression study

Read the full analysis (PDF) Download the Excel workbook

Question

Does average humidity affect offensive performance in Major League Baseball, and does any effect survive once other environmental, ballpark, and roster factors are controlled for? The tension is physical versus physiological: humid air is less dense, so a batted ball meets less resistance and can carry farther — but humidity also raises fatigue, dehydration, and cardiovascular strain on hitters. This study tests whether, on net, humidity moves a team's home weighted on-base average (wOBA).

Data

A team-season panel covering all 30 MLB teams across the 2021–2024 seasons. The dependent variable is home wOBA (mean 0.318); the independent variable of interest is average humidity (mean 0.682, i.e. 68.2%). wOBA is used over older metrics like batting average or OPS because it captures total offensive production in a single number without favoring a particular type of hitter.

  • Dependent variable: Home wOBA
  • Variable of interest: Average humidity percentage
  • Controls: average fan attendance, hitting-coach years of service, average ballpark dimension, average temperature, windspeed, position-player payroll, and sprint speed
  • Dropped: ballpark elevation (multicollinear with humidity and temperature) and total home plate appearances (severe endogeneity)

Sources:

  • Baseball Savant (Statcast) — wOBA and sprint speed
  • Weather World database — humidity, wind speed, and temperature
  • Ballparks of Baseball — ballpark dimensions
  • Sports Library — ballpark elevation
  • ESPN — average fan attendance
  • Spotrac — position-player payroll
  • Hitting-coach years of service — researched individually per team

Methodology

I estimate the relationship with ordinary least squares (OLS) regression across five specifications — a baseline model plus alternative specifications that test the robustness of the results. The general form of the regression is:

wOBAit = β0 + β1 Humidityit + γXit + εit

where X is the set of controls (average fan attendance, hitting-coach years of service, average temperature, and average ballpark dimension). Fan attendance is scaled by dividing by 1,000 so its coefficient is readable. The baseline is then extended with four alternative specifications: adding position-player payroll and sprint speed; adding annual fixed effects; replacing payroll with its natural logarithm; and removing the top and bottom ten wOBA outliers. Standard errors appear in parentheses beneath each coefficient.

Findings

The headline result is a null finding: average humidity does not have a statistically significant effect on offensive production once relevant controls are included.

  • Humidity's apparent effect is omitted-variable bias. Humidity looks significant at the α = .05 level in the early baseline models, but the moment average temperature is added, the humidity coefficient loses significance (p = 0.125). Temperature captures environmental variation that humidity was standing in for.
  • The null result holds everywhere. Across all four alternative specifications — adding payroll and sprint speed, annual fixed effects, log payroll, and removing wOBA outliers — humidity stays insignificant. Its estimated effect is statistically indistinguishable from zero.
  • Average fan attendance is a consistently positive, highly significant predictor (α = .01 in most specifications) — larger home crowds line up with higher home offensive output.
  • Average temperature is positive and significant at α = .01 once included (easing to α = .05 when wOBA outliers are removed) — warmer conditions associate with more offense.
  • Payroll matters only in log form. Raw position-player payroll is insignificant, but its natural logarithm is significant at α = .05 — consistent with diminishing returns to spending.
  • Hitting-coach experience and ballpark dimension were not significant in any specification.

Taken together, the likely interpretation is that the physical benefit of less-dense humid air is offset by the physiological toll humidity places on hitters — leaving no net effect on offensive production.
